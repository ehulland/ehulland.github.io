Growing pockets of under-vaccination contribute to the resurgence and spread of infectious diseases, as demonstrated by an ongoing measles outbreak in northwest Texas. Measles is one of the most contagious diseases known to humans, with a basic reproduction number (R(0)) of 11–18; this means that — in a completely susceptible, unvaccinated population — an individual with measles typically infects an average of 11–18 other people. Therefore, high levels of immunity in a population (approximately 92-94%) are necessary to prevent onward transmission.

We employed the EpiEstim package in R to estimate the current effective reproduction number (R(t)) in Texas. To that end, we extracted daily counts of all measles cases in northwest Texas, as reported in news media and health department websites or archives. To parameterize the EpiEstim model, we used two mean values of the serial interval (SI) — i.e., the time to symptom onset between two consecutive cases — extracted from the published literature (9 and 14.5 days; standard deviation, SD: 3.25 days), a prior mean value for R(0) of 14.5 (SD: 1.5), and a rolling weekly time interval. Posterior estimates of R(t) (2.5th, 50th, and 97.5th percentiles) were retrieved to estimate the time-varying vaccination coverage, VC(t), with a conservative estimate of vaccine effectiveness (VE) in children set to 95%.

We identified a persistent gap in MMR coverage: in the exposed population, early estimates suggested vaccination rates of 40% to 65%, rising steadily thereafter to 79% to 90%. This initial gap likely allowed the measles outbreak to propagate in northwest Texas and to spread more widely. Notably, any concurrent changes in health behavior also induce changes in R(t); as such, these estimates may be conservative. Efforts to rapidly improve MMR supply and coverage are critical to curb the spread in Texas and beyond.
